Federal legislative proposal

Families First Act of 2027

A bill to update and improve the fair distribution of Federal child support incentive payments to States based on updated and improved metrics. It makes a narrow amendment to section 458 of the Social Security Act (42 U.S.C. 658a).

Summary

Current law
Each year HHS divides a fixed, CPI-indexed pool ($755.4 million in FY2026) among States. A State’s share is its total child support collections multiplied by five performance scores, so larger collections earn a larger share.
Proposed change
Replace total collections with an order-adjusted base: a benchmark amount for each month a case has current support owed, minus the total current support owed. Drop the cost-effectiveness score, the only one tied to dollars collected.
What stays the same
The size of the pool; 66 percent federal reimbursement of program costs; State guidelines for support amounts; the paternity, order-establishment, collection-rate, and arrears scores; and the related penalty system.
Federal cost
No change to direct incentive spending, because the pool is unchanged. The proposal redistributes the pool among States. CBO has not estimated it.
Law affected
Social Security Act §§ 458(b) and new 458(g); § 455(a)(4)(C)(iii) (conforming). Committees of jurisdiction: Ways and Means (House) and Finance (Senate).

The proposal removes a federal financial stake in larger support obligations. It does not claim, and the evidence does not establish, that the current incentive changes how courts set support or decide custody.

How the formula works

Order-adjusted base = max(0, B × case-months − current support owed)

State payment = Pool × (base × performance multiplier) ÷ (sum for all States)

Weights are not dollars. Because the national pool is fixed, one more dollar of support owed lowers a State’s actual payment by about one cent, not one dollar. Cases with no current order add nothing, so a State gains nothing by keeping them open.

Open issues

  • The benchmark amount ([$1,000]) and first fiscal year ([2030]) are bracketed policy choices.
  • HHS does not yet collect monthly counts of cases with current support owed; its reporting form and regulations would need revision.
  • States with higher average orders, usually higher-income States, would lose share (New Jersey −63 percent in the simulation). An income adjustment or phase-in is an option.
  • States could respond with lower guideline schedules, more $0 orders, or changes in case handling. The bill keeps existing guideline rules and requires a GAO evaluation.
  • All FY2022–FY2025 data are preliminary, and no CBO estimate exists.
